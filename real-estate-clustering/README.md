# Real Estate Spatial & Price Clustering

Geographical housing hotspot discovery on the **California Housing Prices**
dataset, using **DBSCAN** and **K-Means**, with interactive Folium maps and
Plotly charts.

The project analyses 20,640 census block groups, compares the two clustering
algorithms on identical feature matrices, and explains when each one is the
right tool. Every number in the notebooks and in this README comes from running
the code on the real dataset — nothing is hard-coded or simulated.

---

## 1. Dataset

**Source:** [California Housing Prices on Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/camnugent/california-housing-prices)

| property | value |
|---|---|
| rows | 20,640 census block groups |
| columns | 10 |
| missing values | 207 (`total_bedrooms`, 1.00%) |
| duplicates | 0 |
| price range | \$14,999 – \$500,001 (965 rows capped at the maximum) |

The file is included at `data/housing.csv`. If it is missing, run the notebooks
and `preprocessing.load_data()` will raise a clear error explaining where to
place the file. To download it yourself:

1. Open the Kaggle link above.
2. Download `housing.csv` (a Kaggle account may be required).
3. Place it at `data/housing.csv`.

Or with the Kaggle CLI:

```bash
kaggle datasets download -d camnugent/california-housing-prices
unzip california-housing-prices.zip housing.csv -d data/
```

## 6. Key preprocessing decisions

- **Missing `total_bedrooms` (207 rows):** imputed with the median of the
  block group's `ocean_proximity` group (group medians 423–512; global median
  435). This keeps coastal/inland differences instead of flattening them.
- **Duplicates:** none found, but the pipeline removes them defensively.
- **Engineered features:**
  `rooms_per_household`, `bedrooms_per_room`, `population_per_household`.
  These describe housing character instead of district size.
- **Ratio outliers:** winsorised at the 1st/99th percentiles (414 values per
  ratio, 2% of rows). For example, `population_per_household` falls from an
  impossible 1,243.33 to 5.40. Raw counts are not clipped because very large
  totals are genuine dense districts.
- **Capped prices:** the 965 rows at \$500,001 are top-coded, not errors, and are
  kept — they are largely the expensive coast.
- **Scaling:** `StandardScaler` is essential. The raw Euclidean distance between
  two neighbouring rows is 6,706 because `total_rooms` dominates; after
  standardisation the same pair is 5.21 units apart and the dimensions
  contribute comparably.

### Two feature sets

| set | features | purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `geo` | longitude, latitude | **geographical hotspots**; distances are interpretable |
| `geo_housing` | geo + age, rooms, bedrooms, population, households, income + 3 ratios (11 features) | literal "geographical and housing features" configuration |

`median_house_value` is **not** used as a clustering input by default: clustering
on price and then reporting "average price by cluster" would be circular. It is
the interpretation target, and it is available in the `geo_housing_value`
sensitivity set.

## 7. Results

### 7.1 Algorithm comparison (identical feature matrices)

| metric | DBSCAN (geo) | K-Means (geo) | DBSCAN (11-D) | K-Means (11-D) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| clusters | 22 | 6 | 3 (one holds 98.63%) | 5 |
| noise | 1,252 (6.07%) | 0 | 270 (1.31%) | 0 |
| silhouette | 0.377 | **0.570** | 0.642* | 0.214 |
| Davies–Bouldin (lower better) | 0.495 | 0.689 | **0.370*** | 1.297 |
| Calinski–Harabasz (higher better) | 39,605 | **87,008** | 148* | 4,755 |
| runtime (single run) | ≈0.3 s | ≈0.1–0.25 s | ≈5–6 s | ≈0.1–0.25 s |

\* The 11-D DBSCAN scores are **not** evidence of a good solution: 98.63% of the
data sits in one cluster, so the metrics describe a degenerate partition.

### 7.2 What DBSCAN found (geo, `eps=0.06`, `min_samples=30`)

| cluster | location | block groups | mean value | mean income |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 | Los Angeles / southern coast | 8,862 | \$230,501 | 4.08 |
| 0 | San Francisco Bay Area | 4,749 | \$264,124 | 4.48 |
| 3 | Sacramento / northern Central Valley | 1,696 | \$130,473 | 3.25 |
| 15 | San Diego | 1,586 | \$197,396 | 3.79 |
| 5 | Fresno / Central Valley | 815 | \$82,291 | 2.85 |
| 8 | Bakersfield | 234 | \$80,999 | 2.83 |
| 17 | Santa Barbara coast | 162 | **\$330,037** | 4.26 |
| 6 | Far northern coast (Eureka) | 97 | \$90,726 | 2.56 |

The four largest clusters contain 82% of all block groups. Noise (6.07%) has a
mean value of \$110,920 and only 1.04% of noise points are ≥ \$400k, versus 8.58%
dataset-wide — DBSCAN's noise is mostly remote low-density country, not hidden
luxury.

### 7.3 What K-Means found (geo, K=6)

| region | block groups | mean value | share ≥ \$400k |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| San Francisco Bay Area | 4,827 | \$262,986 | **14.0%** |
| Los Angeles / southern coast | 8,839 | \$230,302 | **10.7%** |
| San Diego | 2,216 | \$177,336 | 4.7% |
| Sacramento / north | 2,328 | \$126,066 | 0.3% |
| Central Valley south | 1,744 | \$123,075 | 2.2% |
| Far north / rural | 686 | \$92,311 | 0.0% |

### 7.4 Analytical answers

- **Where are the hotspots?** Los Angeles/Southern California and the Bay Area
  are the two dominant dense, expensive regions; San Diego and Sacramento are
  the next-largest dense regions; the Central Valley is dense but inexpensive;
  mountain/desert areas are mostly DBSCAN noise.
- **Do expensive properties cluster geographically?** Yes. 14.0% of Bay Area and
  10.7% of Los Angeles block groups are ≥ \$400k, against 0.3% in Sacramento and
  0.0% in the far north. The dataset-wide share is 8.58%.
- **Are clusters associated with income?** Strongly: cluster-level mean income
  and mean value correlate at **r ≈ 0.91** across the 22 DBSCAN clusters and
  **r ≈ 0.99** across the six K-Means regions.
- **How does DBSCAN distinguish dense from isolated?** Points without 30
  neighbours within `eps=0.06` become noise: 1,252 block groups (6.07%).
- **What does K-Means add?** A complete, gap-free regional label for every block
  group, including low-density areas DBSCAN discards, plus attribute segments in
  the 11-D space.
- **Which is more suitable?** DBSCAN on geography for hotspot cores and
  isolation detection; K-Means when a full regional assignment is required.
  Neither is universally better. Running DBSCAN on the 11-D matrix is not
  advisable without feature weighting: nine attribute dimensions overwhelm the
  two geographic ones and the solution collapses into one dominant cluster
  (98.63%).

## 8. A key methodological finding

DBSCAN on the literal "geography + housing characteristics" matrix **fails**:
20,357 of 20,640 points (98.63%) form one cluster. In 11 standardised
dimensions, block groups hundreds of kilometres apart still look similar in age,
size and income, so density is dominated by attributes rather than location.

The notebooks therefore report both configurations honestly:

- **Part A** of notebook 03 shows the failure with the requested 11-feature
  matrix.
- **Part B** restricts DBSCAN to scaled geography, which is what "geographical
  hotspot" actually means, and obtains 22 meaningful clusters.
- Notebook 05 compares both algorithms on **both** matrices, so the comparison
  remains like-for-like.

This is a genuine result from the data, not a parameter-tuning trick.

---

## 9. A structural difference between the algorithms

A synthetic two-moons experiment (clearly labelled as synthetic in notebook 05)
isolates cluster-shape sensitivity:

| algorithm | Adjusted Rand Index |
|---|---:|
| DBSCAN | **1.000** |
| K-Means | 0.249 |

DBSCAN follows density and recovers both crescents; K-Means can only cut the
plane into convex regions. The same contrast appears in the housing map as
DBSCAN's long connected coastal corridors versus K-Means' compact regions.

## 11. Limitations

- Data are **1990 census block groups**, not individual transactions.
- `median_house_value` is top-coded at \$500,001, which compresses the high end.
- Euclidean distance ignores roads, coastline barriers, and spatial
  autocorrelation; no spatial weights or GIS layers are used.
- Cluster labels describe this dataset; they are not causal claims about prices.
- Silhouette is computed on a reproducible 8,000-point sample and excludes
  DBSCAN noise, so it must always be read together with coverage/noise.
- Runtimes are single-run measurements and vary with machine load.

## 12. Final conclusion

The California housing market in this dataset is organised around a small number
of dense, high-income coastal corridors. DBSCAN on geography cleanly separates
those corridors from isolated rural block groups and identifies the Santa
Barbara coast as the most expensive dense pocket (\$330,037 mean). K-Means gives
a smoother, complete six-region map in which the Bay Area and Los Angeles
account for most ≥ \$400k housing.

The most important lesson is methodological: **feature-space design matters as
much as the algorithm**. DBSCAN is excellent for irregular, density-defined
hotspots in low-dimensional geographic space, but collapses in the 11-D
attribute space. K-Means is fast and always produces a usable partition, yet it
cannot say "this location belongs to no cluster" and cannot represent non-convex
shapes. Using both — DBSCAN for cores and anomalies, K-Means for complete
regional coverage — is more informative than choosing one as universally
"better".
